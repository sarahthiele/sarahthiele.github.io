Research

My research sits at the intersection of observational astronomy and space sustainability, spanning satellite trails, near-Earth objects, and space policy.

Current · PhD Research

Satellite Trail Detection & Characterization in HATPI

Wide-field telescopes like HATPI are increasingly affected by satellite trails crossing their images. I developed and maintain a pipeline for detecting, measuring, and identifying these trails in HATPI data from Las Campanas Observatory. The pipeline combines a neural network with a post-processing scheme and photometry measurements to empirically study trends in the numbers and brightness of satellite trails over time.

This work feeds directly into the broader question of how the proliferation of satellite constellations is changing the observational landscape for ground-based astronomy, and allows us to mitigate their effects on our science.

Current · PhD Research

Near-Earth Objects and Planetary Defense

The next phase of my thesis extends the satellite-trail work outward, from low Earth orbit to the near-Earth object population. Wide-field surveys see streaks from both populations — an artificial satellite and a fast-moving asteroid passing close to Earth can leave remarkably similar signatures on a detector. My machine learning pipeline, originally built to find and characterize satellite trails in HATPI imagery, is well positioned to make that distinction, separating manmade objects from genuine solar system movers rather than treating every streak as contamination to be masked out.

With HATPI's continuous coverage of the full visible sky from Las Campanas Observatory, I plan to use this capability to monitor for nearby, fast-moving small asteroids: the population that is closest to us, moves too quickly for many conventional surveys, and is most relevant to planetary defense. Beyond detection, the goal is characterization — deriving physical and orbital properties for objects that currently have few or no such measurements, and turning a survey designed for variable stars and transits into a tool for small-body science.

Published · Acta Astronautica · 2026

An Orbital House of Cards: Frequent Satellite Close Conjunctions

We introduce the "CRASH Clock," a metric that quantifies stress on the orbital environment by measuring how long, absent satellite maneuvers, until a catastrophic collision might occur. As of June 2025 the clock stood at 5.5 days and continues to fall — down from 164 days in 2018.

This decline tracks the rapid growth of satellite megaconstellations, the accumulation of space debris, and periods of degraded situational awareness, such as during solar storms. Published in Acta Astronautica; preprint on arXiv.

Published · Journal of the Astronautical Sciences · 2022

Space Weapon Debris + Satellite Constellations

We modelled the debris clouds generated by kinetic anti-satellite (ASAT) tests — counterspace demonstrations that destroy satellites in Low Earth Orbit. Integrating debris fragment lifetimes against future LEO environments densely populated by satellite constellations, we find a potentially significant likelihood of debris fragments striking operational satellites.

The analysis code, JunkySpace, is available on GitHub. The paper was originally presented at the 2021 AMOS Conference and subsequently published in the Journal of the Astronautical Sciences.

Published · The Astrophysical Journal · 2023

Stellar Metallicity and the LISA-Observable Double White Dwarf Population

The binary fraction of solar-type stars in the Milky Way is anti-correlated with stellar metallicity. I investigated the impact of this metallicity-dependent binary fraction on the Galactic population of double white dwarf (DWD) binaries, simulating Milky Way-like galaxies and computing their gravitational wave signals as future sources for the space-based detector LISA.

Depending on the binary evolution model, the LISA-observable Galactic DWD population may be reduced by more than half when accounting for this effect — with significant consequences for LISA science forecasts. Published in ApJ.

LIGO Scientific Collaboration · 2022–2025

LIGO Detector Characterization

Before a gravitational-wave signal can be trusted, it has to be distinguished from instrumental noise. I contributed to detector characterization efforts within the LIGO Scientific Collaboration, working on the identification, modeling, and classification of transient noise artifacts ("glitches") that can mimic or mask real astrophysical signals.

  • LIGO Detector Characterization in the First Half of the Fourth Observing Run — Classical and Quantum Gravity, 2025. A collaboration-wide summary of detector performance, noise mitigation, and data-quality tools developed for O4.
  • Parameterised Population Models of Transient Non-Gaussian Noise in the LIGO Gravitational-Wave Detectors — Classical and Quantum Gravity, 2022. Statistical models of common glitch classes, mapped into the same parameter space as astrophysical signals to help tell the two apart.
  • A New Method to Distinguish Gravitational-Wave Signals from Detector Noise Transients with Gravity Spy — Physical Review D (submitted 2023). A Gravity Spy–based classifier that correctly flagged 100% of confirmed O3b detections as astrophysical.
